# Elemental structure through a peeling cascade

The elemental question requires following the same material through a specified intervention. We distinguish successive ionization at fixed nuclear charge, removal of material from a film or contact, and elimination of coordinates in an effective electronic model. Each can expose a sequence of thresholds, but the state variables and conservation laws differ. A claim that these sequences represent one mechanism requires an explicit mapping supported by measurements.

For Cu, Ag and Au, the first twelve NIST ionization thresholds show a pronounced later increment between the eleventh and twelfth removals. For Pt, the corresponding increment occurs between the tenth and eleventh. This matches the neutral outer-electron counts 11, 11, 11 and 10, respectively. The observation provides an element-resolved calibration of shell structure; several high-charge thresholds are estimated and their source uncertainties are retained. It does not identify atomic ionization thresholds with the interband energy differences of a conducting solid. [NIST ASD](https://physics.nist.gov/asd)

The optical comparison supplies a different, complementary record. Replaying measured Au, Ag and Cu optical constants through Maxwell reflection reveals strongly wavelength-dependent visible reflectance in Au and Cu and high reflectance across the visible range in Ag. A published Pt optical fit supplies a separate, explicitly fitted comparator. Finite-film calculations then show that geometric thinning and substrate changes alter reflection, absorption, transmission and calculated color even when the material constants are held fixed. The interface therefore belongs in the observation model alongside the electronic structure. [Johnson & Christy](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.6.4370), [Rakić et al.](https://doi.org/10.1364/AO.37.005271)

The recovered hypersurface construction provides an elementary common model. For Hθ = a(cosθ σz + sinθ σx) and a fixed dipole D = σz, the levels remain ±a while the squared interband dipole is sin²θ. Coupling the two fixed basis orbitals to separate wide-band leads of width γ gives, at zero probe energy, G/G₀ = γ²a²sin²θ/(a² + γ²/4)². Optical strength and current thus vary together under an orbital rotation at fixed spacing. This is a conditional relation for declared contacts and dipole, rather than an ab initio calculation for the four elements. The accompanying ideal-band color calculation connects to recent work on quantum-geometric optical conductivity. [Oh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18372)

The cascade must also retain feedback. Eliminating a side orbital leaves the self-energy g²/(E−ed). Reducing this coupling can increase the retained conductance by releasing destructive interference; in the present control it rises from 0.180144 to one conductance quantum. This does not violate monotonicity under removal of fixed nonnegative contributions, because the remaining scattering amplitudes change. The Schur boundary operator in the hypersurface formulation expresses the same reduction principle: an eliminated sector can continue to affect boundary observations through its retained self-energy.

Electrical channel identification supplies a further constraint. G and shot noise determine two moments of the transmission eigenvalues, which can distinguish a single open channel from two half-open channels but generally cannot uniquely identify three or more channels. Published Au/Ag/Pt atomic-contact work makes the elemental distinction experimentally relevant, while recent gold-contact thermopower measurements show why energy-sensitive and temperature-dependent observables must be retained even on a conductance plateau. [Vardimon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1308.3425), [Möller et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.12734v1)

These results motivate an operational elemental-peel hypothesis: a validated electronic and boundary model should predict coordinated changes in spacing-sensitive, coupling-sensitive and transport observables along the same measured intervention. The present supplement establishes a reproducible calibration using evaluated atomic data, measured optical constants, fitted optical response and exact finite-model controls. Testing a material removal law requires a geometry-calibrated trajectory with electrical and optical measurements; it remains the next physical experiment rather than a conclusion of this calculation.

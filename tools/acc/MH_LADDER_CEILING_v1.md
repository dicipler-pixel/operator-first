# Where the corridor-ladder method runs out

Measured per-cap component growth on the two fully-laddered roots.

## Growth factor

| R6 | | R7 | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 21→22 | ×3.91 | 23→24 | ×3.90 |
| 22→23 | **×11.85** | 24→25 | **×13.03** |
| 23→24 | ×4.13 | 25→26 | ×4.27 |
| 24→25 | ×4.24 | 26→27 | ×4.50 |
| 25→26 | ×3.92 | 27→28 | ×4.12 |
| 26→27 | ×3.68 | 28→29 | ×3.85 |
| 27→28 | **×3.55** | 29→30 | **×3.71** |

Two features, both consistent across the roots:

- a single **spike** at the third rung, ×11.9 and ×13.0, where the component
  jumps two orders of magnitude in one cap — a structural transition early in
  the ladder, not a gradual opening;
- a **slowly declining trailing factor**, 4.24 → 3.92 → 3.68 → 3.55 on R6 and
  4.50 → 4.12 → 3.85 → 3.71 on R7. Growth is a little sub-geometric, so the
  projections below are mildly pessimistic.

## Projection and the memory wall

The engine holds ~45 bytes per state (40-byte packed state, 4-byte parent,
1-byte move) plus a power-of-two open-addressed index table at 4 bytes per
slot, sized to at least twice the state count.

| root | next cap | projected states | memory | reachable at 15 GB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R6 | 29 | ~226M | ~11.5 GB | **yes, barely** |
| R6 | 30 | ~757M | ~39.7 GB | no |
| R6 | 31 | ~2,454M | ~134.8 GB | no |
| R7 | 31 | ~369M | ~19.5 GB | **no** |
| R7 | 32 | ~1,288M | ~70.0 GB | no |
| R7 | 33 | ~4,365M | ~247.0 GB | no |

Both projections are confirmed against runs already made: R6 at cap 29 with
**200,000,000** forward states returned BUDGET (depth 47, no path ≤ 54),
which is exactly what a ~226M component predicts. R7 at cap 31 under the
same 200M allocation cannot close either, needing ~369M.

## Consequence

**The ladder method is at its ceiling on this hardware.** R6 may take one
further cap with a squeeze to ~250M states, which would move its wall to
total length ≥ 30. R7 cannot advance at all. Past that the limit is memory,
not budget or patience, and every additional cap costs another ×3.6.

Further progress on these two roots therefore needs a different method, not
a larger allocation. The lower-n roots are the opposite case: n=8, 9 and 10
stopped at their first BUDGET under a 25M sweep allocation while their next
caps project to only ~34M, ~45M and ~59M, so those walls are floors that
ordinary budget will lift.

## A quotient that does not exist

A tempting lever: the by-4 theorem says moves 0 and 1 generate V = Z/2 x Z/2
acting **freely** on every component, so 4 divides every component size.
Quotienting the search by V would give a 4x state reduction, worth about one
extra cap.

**It is unsound.** A quotient needs V to act by *graph automorphisms* —
every V-image of an edge must itself be an edge — and it does not. Tested
over 10,080 edges from two starts: **1,920 (19%) of V-images are not single-
move edges**.

    s     = ((x,x,y,X,y), (y,X,y))    --move 2-->  t = ((x,x,y,X,y,y,X,y), (y,X,y))
    V1(s) = ((Y,x,Y,X,X), (y,X,y))
    V1(t) = ((Y,x,Y,Y,x,Y,X,X), (y,X,y))          no single move connects them

The reason is structural: the multiplication moves are **right**
multiplications. Inverting r0 sends `r0*r1` to `r1^-1 * r0^-1`, a left
multiplication, which is not in `ac-r2-v1`.

The by-4 theorem is unaffected — V is generated by two actual moves, so
V-images lie in the same component, and the action is free. But "acts freely
within the component" is strictly weaker than "acts by automorphisms", and
only the second licenses a quotient.

So the only real memory lever is the narrow state: words of at most 32
letters need one uint64 each rather than two, and closure-only runs need no
parent or move arrays, taking 45+8 bytes per state down to 24+8. That is
**1.65x**, worth **~0.39 caps** at a per-cap factor of 3.6 — not the ~1.6
caps a 4x quotient would have bought.

The ceiling therefore stands as first measured: R6 may reach cap 29 with a
squeeze, R7 cannot advance, and the next real move is an invariant that
separates component from target outright — an unrestricted one, where the
external restricted trace lemma is a kernel-constrained special case.

## Correction: growth is not a stable constant

The projections above used a trailing factor of ~3.6 and described growth as
"stable and slowly declining". That was read off the **upper rungs of R6 and
R7 only**. Lifting the remaining sweep floors produced a counterexample:

    R_4  cap 22 -> 23:   2,064,600 -> 92,052,208    x44.59

Projected ~8M; actual 92M, wrong by 11x. All seventeen measured factors:

| root | factors |
|---|---|
| R_4 | **x44.59** |
| R_6 | x3.91  **x11.85**  x4.13  x4.24  x3.92  x3.68  x3.55 |
| R_7 | x3.90  **x13.03**  x4.27  x4.50  x4.12  x3.85  x3.71 |
| R_8 | x4.01 |
| R_9 | x4.16 |

min x3.55, median x4.12, max **x44.59** — a 13x spread. The x11.85 and x13.03
spikes on R6/R7 were recorded earlier and dismissed as an early structural
transition; R_4 shows a far larger spike much later in its ladder, so that
reading was wrong. Spikes are a feature of these ladders, not an artifact of
where a ladder starts.

**Consequence for the projections.** The memory estimates for R6 cap 30
(~757M) and R7 cap 31 (~369M) assumed the trailing factor continues. They are
therefore **lower bounds on the true cost**, not estimates — a spike would put
either far beyond them. The ceiling conclusion is unaffected in direction and
is in fact stronger than argued: those caps are at least as unreachable as
stated, possibly much more so.

What cannot be done from this data is predict *which* rung spikes. R_4's came
at its second measured rung, R6's and R7's at their third. Nothing measured so
far distinguishes a spiking rung in advance.

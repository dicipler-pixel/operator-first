# Win long AC records through global route diversity

**Plain-language summary**

- **Nobody at the top of the SAIR board has said publicly how they find their short paths.** What is public comes from mid-table teams, published papers and open-source code. The best public solver still produces paths about **twice as long** as the leaders' on long instances.
- **On long instances, local polishing stops paying off quickly.** If you tidy up small stretches of a path that is already decent, you gain little. Our own exact window-14 pass shortened 2 of 9 paths by 1–2 moves. The strongest public team found no gain at all from a similar local pass. Record paths take a *different route overall*; they are not a tidied-up version of ours.
- **The gains that other teams measured came from having many different paths and recombining them.** They restart the search from points along the best path so far ("waypoints"), cut and join pieces of different paths ("splicing"), and look for any path state that has a known-short route to the finish.
- **Making the beam wider does not help.** One team tried beams of 60,000 and 100,000 states and saw no improvement. The search is limited by *which* states it keeps, not *how many*.
- **A smarter scoring rule is the cheapest likely win.** The Caltech group tuned a score from five simple features of the two relators. On their benchmark it cut the mean path from 142.6 to 102.7 moves and the median from 46.5 to 38.
- **Our beam fails above record ~50 because of how it is used, not because it is weak.** Capping depth at record+2 asks it to find a near-record path in a single pass. The better approach is two phases: first find *any* path with no depth cap, then shorten it by restarts, splicing and exact finishing searches.
- **Machine learning is low priority for a 4-core machine.** Public attempts (learned heuristics, expert iteration, AlphaZero-style self-play) did not beat a plain length rule at small scale.

---

On 100–200-move instances, the short-path methods that have measured results all do the same thing: they **generate many diverse routes and recombine them globally**, rather than optimising one path locally. For our setup the ranked plan is as follows. First, turn `acc_beam` into a two-phase tool with no depth cap during discovery and a tuned multi-feature priority. Next, add waypoint restarts and cross-path splicing on top of the existing exact tools (`acc_capped` + T_9 and `tail_replace.py`). Then use symmetry relabelings of each instance as a free source of route diversity. Only after that consider a larger endgame table or longer exact windows. The evidence is thin, and that matters. The competition launched on 11 September 2026 and closes on 30 November 2026. The dominant teams (Dirac, NavierStoked, JDD) have published nothing. Tao's blog comments, the SAIR Zulip and social media were unreachable when these notes were compiled. The recommendations therefore rest on two candid mid-table write-ups, the Caltech ACSolverX code and data, and transferable practice from permutation-puzzle competitions and automated planning. That foundation is good enough to act on. It is not good enough to promise record-beating paths.

## Only outright shortest paths score, and the leaders are about 2× ahead on long rows

The Discovery Track poses **10,115 two-generator balanced presentations**, each scored on an AC board and a Stable AC board. The **k teams tied at the shortest verified path each earn 2^(1−k)** ([rules](https://github.com/SAIRcompetition/Andrews-Curtis/blob/main/competition/rules/discovery.md)). The AC endpoint must be exactly `(x, y)` in that order. There are 14 move IDs: relator inversion, right-multiplication by the other relator or its inverse, and conjugation by x^{±1} or y^{±1}. Paths may be up to 100,000 moves long, and total relator length may reach 10,000 ([rules](https://github.com/SAIRcompetition/Andrews-Curtis/blob/main/competition/rules/discovery.md)). Short records are crowded: holds are shared by up to **16 teams**, and at k = 12 a tie is worth about 0.0005 points ([Amey campaign log](https://github.com/Amey-Thakur/SAIR-ANDREWS-CURTIS-CHALLENGE/blob/main/runs/campaign_log.md)). The points are therefore on long rows where one team holds the record outright. That matches our focus on 100–200-move instances.

Two public data points show how far ahead the leaders are. Team nahom finished 10th on AC and 9th on Stable AC with a serious C++ solver. They report that **"our best paths are typically about 2× the leaders' record lengths on longer instances"** ([nahomar README](https://github.com/nahomar/andrews-curtis-solver)). The Amey team held 144 AC challenges, all of them shared ties. It fell from rank 7 on 12 September to **rank 22 (0.037 points) by 25 September** ([Amey discovery-track README](https://github.com/Amey-Thakur/SAIR-ANDREWS-CURTIS-CHALLENGE/blob/main/discovery-track/README.md)). On 12 September, Dirac was gaining roughly **325 outright holds per hour** ([campaign log](https://github.com/Amey-Thakur/SAIR-ANDREWS-CURTIS-CHALLENGE/blob/main/runs/campaign_log.md)). A team named "AC-Solver =D" entered at rank 4 on 16 September. That is the name of the reinforcement-learning solver of Shehper et al., which suggests the Caltech group or its associates are competing ([campaign log](https://github.com/Amey-Thakur/SAIR-ANDREWS-CURTIS-CHALLENGE/blob/main/runs/campaign_log.md)). The Amey team's belief that the leaders run RL-guided solvers is a competitor's speculation, not a disclosed method. The honest position is that **leader methods are unknown**. The plausible ingredients are large compute, macro moves, splicing and post-processing, and possibly learned guidance.

For comparison, the research literature reaches long paths through several routes. Greedy best-first search on total relator length solved 533 of 1,190 Miller–Schupp presentations, and its paths run up to **345 moves** ([AC-Solver](https://github.com/shehper/AC-Solver)). An RL-assisted path of **381 moves** appears in Shehper et al. ([arXiv 2408.15332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15332)). Prover9 produced an **8,634-move** trivialization after 74 days ([Lisitsa 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666657X2500028X)). None of that work optimised for *short* paths, so the competition itself is the frontier for this question.

## Local exact polishing saturates, as three independent results show

Our own evidence and the public evidence agree. Our exact window-14 shortener (`research/acc/optimality/acc_window.c`) decides d(s_i, s_j) exactly for every pair within 14 steps. Across 9 near-record paths it shortened only **ac-05558 (32→30) and ac-02628 (28→27)**. The other seven were already locally optimal (`tools/acc/WARROOM_2026-09-24_window14.md`). Team nahom tried a radius-5/6 local shortcut pass and got **no gain**, concluding that **"record routes differ globally → conversion needs route diversity"** ([nahom experiments](https://github.com/nahomar/andrews-curtis-solver/blob/main/solver/README_EXPERIMENTS.md)). The Amey team's weighted A\*, IDA\* and decision-mode IDA\* scored **zero** on records of length 21–45, with paths about 10% longer than the records ([Amey README](https://github.com/Amey-Thakur/SAIR-ANDREWS-CURTIS-CHALLENGE)).

Beam width shows the same limit. nahom ran beams of **60,000 and 100,000** states with no gain: "Width is fully saturated" ([experiments](https://github.com/nahomar/andrews-curtis-solver/blob/main/solver/README_EXPERIMENTS.md)). This explains our `acc_beam` failure. Capping depth at record+2 on a record above 50 asks a width-limited beam to land on a near-optimal route in one pass. Width alone cannot find that route. Exact search has a hard ceiling too. Our T_9 holds **214,977,551** states and needed 167.8 s to build, and it certifies only the last nine moves (`tools/acc/WARROOM_2026-09-24_optimality_R15.md`). An exact meet at 100+ moves is out of reach by many orders of magnitude.

Permutation-puzzle and planning practice points the same way. The Kaggle Santa 2023 winners trimmed loops and re-searched neighbourhoods around each state, but *only on short subsequences, repeated many times*. Full-graph optimisation worked only on small puzzles ([maddevs write-up](https://maddevs.io/writeups/polytope-permutation-puzzle/)). In planning, the classical post-processors are Action Elimination and Plan Neighborhood Graph Search (PNGS). PNGS builds a graph from the states around an existing plan and extracts a shortest path through it ([Nakhost & Müller, ICAPS 2010](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220936291_Action_Elimination_and_Plan_Neighborhood_Graph_Search_Two_Algorithms_for_Plan_Improvement)). Our window tool is PNGS on a single path. The missing piece is PNGS over **many** paths.

## Diversity plus recombination produced the only measured gains

nahom's log is the only public ablation study of shortening on real competition rows, and it is specific:

| Technique (nahom, 23–25 Sep) | Measured effect | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Rebeam: restart the beam from waypoints at 20/35/50/65/80% of the best path, then splice | **1636 → 1574** total moves (−3.8%), 9 of 15 rows shorter | Adopted |
| Exact "ball" of all states up to total relator length 17 (513.6M states, 9.7 GB) | **1596 → 1579** on 13 rows (−1.1%) | Adopted |
| Class-distance oracle (21.2M classes to length 22) at low weight | +5% overall, but better on some rows and solves some never-solved rows | Adopted for diversity |
| Cross-puzzle transfer AC↔SAC | 127 + 127 paths improved, none to record | Marginal |
| Macros mined from near-record paths | "Dilutes the beam" | Rejected |
| Learned linear heuristic | 1368 → **2044** (worse; distribution shift) | Rejected |
| Expert iteration / pretraining on 400k exact distances | 0.29–0.32 vs 0.371 best-sibling accuracy of shortest-first | Parked |
| AlphaZero-style PUCT self-play | Learns (69%→94% at k = 8); paths equal, none shorter | Not yet useful |

Source: [nahom README_EXPERIMENTS](https://github.com/nahomar/andrews-curtis-solver/blob/main/solver/README_EXPERIMENTS.md).

These results rank the techniques. Waypoint restarts with splicing give the largest single gain, and they are exactly the "global route diversity" the local passes lack. A restart from 50% of the way along the path explores a different region than the original beam. Splicing the best continuation onto the prefix gives a new path. Doing this repeatedly across waypoints is a cheap form of iterated local search. The exact endgame ball helps less than one might expect. The Amey team found that a single global ball capped at total length 40 (22M states) contained only **2 of the 10,115** starting presentations. Per-challenge caps work; global balls do not ([campaign log](https://github.com/Amey-Thakur/SAIR-ANDREWS-CURTIS-CHALLENGE/blob/main/runs/campaign_log.md)). Our T_9 already covers the depth-based version of this idea. Note that nahom's ball is organised by relator length, not depth. It contains every presentation of total length ≤17 with its exact distance to the goal. That makes it a "landing zone": the path finishes as soon as it touches any short state. It does not merely cover the final nine moves.

The search-priority result is the other strong signal. ACSolverX tuned a priority from 17 candidate features: `L + 2.53·K + 6.418·MK + 8.458·S + 3.292·xyimb`. Here L is total length, K is the summed per-relator "knot" count (the number of alternating x/y blocks, read cyclically), MK is the larger of the two relators' knot counts, S is the mean run length of the thinner generator, and xyimb is |#x − #y| / L. On a 60-row benchmark the tuned priority cut the **median path from 46.5 to 38.0 and the mean from 142.6 to 102.7**, and it cut median node count from 1,310 to 227 ([ACSolverX](https://github.com/Avi161/ACSolverX)). One caveat: that benchmark uses substitution supermoves and Miller–Schupp instances, so the path metric is not the official 14-move count. The *direction* of the gain should transfer. Its *size* must be measured on our rows. The same work shows that search cost is **not invariant under relabeling generators**. Two presentations in the same Aut(F₂) class cost 59,710 and 213,882 nodes, because greedy search "reads strings, not orbits" ([ACSolverX](https://github.com/Avi161/ACSolverX)). Relabeling therefore gives diversity almost for free.

## Ranked plan for acc_capped, acc_beam and acc_window on 4 cores

The ranking weighs expected gain on 100–200-move rows against engineering effort, given the tools we already have. "Effort" assumes our existing C kernel (validated with 0 mismatches against the official `core.apply_move`) and Python drivers.

| Rank | Technique | What to build | Expected gain | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Two-phase acc_beam: discover with no depth cap, then shorten** | Remove the record+2 depth cap in discovery. Cap *relator length* instead (e.g. peak + 4–8). Keep the best K distinct complete paths, not just one. | Turns "no path" into a path on every row; this is the prerequisite for everything below | Low |
| 2 | **Tuned multi-feature priority** | Add K, MK, S and xyimb to the acc_beam score, starting from the ACSolverX weights. Re-tune on our own solved rows. | Largest reported shortening (median −18%, mean −28% on their benchmark); unverified on the official metric | Low |
| 3 | **Waypoint restarts + splice ("rebeam")** | For waypoints at 20/35/50/65/80% of the best path, run the beam from s_i and splice any shorter completion. Repeat until nothing improves. | −3.8% total, 9 of 15 rows shorter (nahom) | Low–medium |
| 4 | **tail_replace on every path state** | Already written (`research/acc/optimality/tail_replace.py`). Run it after each rebeam round, with the T_9 finish. | Exact endgame cleanup; sets up gains from rank 3 | Very low |
| 5 | **Symmetry-relabelled restarts** | Run discovery on images of the instance under move-set symmetries (x↔y, x→x⁻¹, y→y⁻¹, relator swap). Map the path back and append the few moves needed to reach exactly (x, y). | Free diversity: search cost varies 3.6× across one orbit ([ACSolverX](https://github.com/Avi161/ACSolverX)) | Low–medium |
| 6 | **Cross-path PNGS / union-graph splice** | Hash every state of every path found (all restarts, beams and relabelings) into one table mapping state → (path, index). Run a shortest-path search over the union graph; add radius-3/4 balls around path states for extra links. | The mechanism behind nahom's adopted "cross-path splicing"; scales with the diversity from ranks 3 and 5 | Medium |
| 7 | **Length-organised endgame ball** | An exact-distance table of all states with total relator length ≤ 16–17 as a landing zone. At 17 it needs about 9.7 GB, which is tight but feasible in 15 GB; at 16 it holds 129M states. | ≈1% (nahom) | Medium |
| 8 | **Loops modulo symmetry** | If s_j = g·s_i for a symmetry g that maps moves to moves and fixes the goal orbit, drop i..j and apply g to the rest of the path. | Small, exact, cheap | Low |
| 9 | **Longer exact windows (16–18)** | Stream one side, e.g. B8∩B8 or B8∩B9 (≈3.4 GB). This costs about 8–50× the time of window-14. | Low: local passes are already saturated | Medium |
| 10 | Substitution supermoves as macros | Implement ACSolverX S-moves for *discovery* only, then expand each into elementary moves. | Helps find paths on hard rows; expanded paths may be long | High |
| 11 | Learned guidance / RL | — | No public evidence of gain at this compute scale | High |

There is a correctness trap under ranks 5 and 8. **A symmetry can be used for shortening only if it maps the 14-move set onto itself and fixes the goal.** Cyclic rotation of a relator is *not* free under the official kernel, because each conjugation move is counted. A general change of variables in Aut(F₂) does not map conjugation-by-x to a single move either. ACSolverX's oracle change of variables halves median path length (**46.5 → 16.5**), but finding the right one costs about **2.2M nodes per presentation** and the result lives in a different move metric ([ACSolverX](https://github.com/Avi161/ACSolverX)). In our setting, use only the eight-to-sixteen move-set symmetries. Charge the cost of the endpoint fix-up honestly. Replay every spliced path with the official `core.verify`, as `tail_replace.py` already does.

On budget: our window-14 run took about 6 s per path state on these rows (for example, a 40-move path took 247.5 s). A full window pass over a 150-move path therefore costs roughly 15 minutes. Discovery beams and rebeams parallelise trivially across the 4 cores, one row or one waypoint per core. The T_9 table (280M slots) should be memory-mapped once and shared across processes, not loaded four times.

## Conclusion

Global route diversity is the lever for long AC rows. Local exactness is not. Every public attempt to squeeze a single decent path locally has stalled: our window-14 pass, nahom's radius-5/6 pass, and Amey's IDA\* variants. Every measured improvement came from generating different routes and splicing them. For us the practical change is small. Stop asking `acc_beam` to hit record+2 in one shot. Let it produce many uncapped paths under a better priority and under relabelings. Then let the exact tools we already trust (`acc_capped` with T_9, `tail_replace.py`, `acc_window`) act as the splice-and-finish layer. Ranks 1–4 need about a day of work and reuse the existing C kernel. They are the right test of whether we can close part of the 2× gap.

Two things remain unknown and should shape expectations. The leaders' methods are undisclosed, and their 2× advantage over a strong public beam solver suggests something qualitatively stronger than any single technique above. Candidates are far more compute, learned guidance at scale, or supermove discovery with aggressive post-processing. Also, the ACSolverX priority gains were measured in a different move metric, and the nahom gains come from 13–15 rows. Both should be re-measured on a fixed panel of our own 100–200-move rows before we commit engineering time beyond rank 4.
